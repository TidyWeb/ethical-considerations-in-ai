## Introduction to Ethics

Welcome to the Data Ethics course, where we will consider the ethics of AI usage.

We will start this lesson by using Robots to represent how humans might interact with artificial intelligence. The primary learning objective is to understand the legal and ethical issues in developing and using artificial intelligence. This objective can be divided into two stages. Firstly, the legal, social and moral issues relating to the development and use of artificial intelligence. Secondly, the rules and principles of using robots in our homes and workplaces, including safety, privacy, and responsibility. These principles will be covered in the following video.

Ethics is the philosophy of recommending a system of right and wrong behaviours. These standards of behaviour may come from our personal views on fairness, societal norms or written laws.

So, what has this got to do with robots? Robots already work alongside humans and do so autonomously. Therefore, they must work in the same ethical framework as we do and must follow the same external rules for ethics. These may be laws or cultural norms. Bribery is outlawed worldwide, but there are cultures where it might not be seen as immoral.

We as humans have our internal ethics, often called morals, that depend on the culture and society in which we were brought up. As ethical standards can change over time and across societies, our internal moral code may differ from the external rules. Therefore, as a developer of robot software, you have to program the robot to make moral choices appropriate to the environment in which it will work.

Robots existed in fiction before reality. Consequently, writers proposed the first ethical rules for robots in science fiction. Isaac Asimov, one of the founding fathers of modern science fiction, devised three laws for robots in a 1942 short story. They are:

1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
2. A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Laws.

Although Asimov suggested these laws for fictional humanoid robot servants, they became very influential as a template for guiding robot ethics development. However, there are many issues with the laws that Asimov himself discussed in later novels. Indeed, he added a zeroth law, so numbered as it is more important than the others.

0. A robot may not harm humanity, or, by inaction, allow humanity to come to harm.

Another problem is that we still do not have artificially intelligent humanoid robots walking through our streets. Robots in real life are far more varied and often basic than those in science fiction. A simple robot with a single task, say to clean a floor, may not require these laws to be explicitly programmed into it as a moral code. A more complicated robot working in the military or a hospital may need these laws to be programmed. Another concern is the vagueness of harm. Does this mean a robot waiter must refuse you a third slice of cake lest it increases your blood sugar?

Asimov’s laws provide founding ethical principles for robots to follow. Still, we should also ask what laws should apply to robot creators as a society.

As robots came into common usage, a need arose for laws governing them. Far Eastern cultures see robots positively and so were early to create legislation for them. In Western cultures, robots are often viewed more negatively. Only in 2017 did the European Union (EU) set out civil law rules for robotics, which resulted in the EU AI Act of 2023. Asimov's laws were used to inspire South Korean and EU robot laws. However, both bodies have legislated for a broader range of current and future robot use cases.

Traditionally, laws have governed human individuals, so why not make autonomous robots legally responsible? Unfortunately, this approach raises as many issues as it solves. Robots are not conscious, so they only make decisions based on their programming or teaching. Liability for the robot’s actions rests with the initial designer, the programmer, the manufacturer and the user, not with the robot. There is always a human behind the scenes.

If you made a robot a legal person, it would have moral obligations that imply it has rights. Can a robot refuse dangerous work and ask for leave or a salary?

So, how should parliaments legislate for robots and, by extension, AI? Who is legally liable for damage caused by a robot? One possibility is an insurance scheme paid for by robot manufacturers and owners to compensate for the damage.

The robot manufacturer is liable for damage due to a mechanical defect or flawed safety system. This liability would also cover software or programming errors. If damage is due to user error, such as using a robot for a purpose not intended, then the user is at fault. If damage occurs during robot teaching, the human teacher would be considered at fault. Whoever owns or leases a robot will also have some responsibility no different from existing laws governing, for example, a car.

The European civil law rules in robotics are only guidelines, so national governments will have to modify their existing laws. The EU AI Act is in place, but the UK government is still in the consultation phase in 2024. Under existing health and safety laws, legislation exists for an industrial robot on a production line. A surgical robot or a drone has an operator, so they are responsible. A partially autonomous car currently requires a licensed driver, but who will be responsible for a fully autonomous car in the future? Some manufacturers, such as Mercedes-Benz and Volvo, have volunteered to take full responsibility for accidents due to their self-driving technology. However, governments and courts will ultimately decide.

In this video, you were introduced to the legal, social and moral issues relating to the development and use of robots. Robots’ ethics and legal obligations rest on the people creating, deploying, and maintaining the robot. The next video will introduce you to the rules and principles of using robots in our homes and workplaces, including safety, privacy, and responsibility. ## Roboethics

Roboethics.

In the previous introduction to robot ethics video, we covered the legal, social and moral issues relating to the development and use of robots.

In this video, we will cover the rules and principles that arise from using robots in our homes and workplaces, including safety, privacy, and responsibility.

This general ethical framework governing robot design and usage is labelled roboethics. It complements the existing human rights legislation and is intended to balance the advantages and risks of robotics to humanity.

Roboethics are ethical standards directed at humankind. It is the responsibility of the designers, producers and users of robots to minimise the risks posed by robots, as even autonomous robots are unable to make moral decisions themselves.

What are the Roboethics principles?

These principles are based on discussions by the European Robotics Research Network (EURON) and the European RoboLaw Consortium to create a code of conduct for robotics. It lays out a code of conduct for robotics designers, developers, and users.

Robots should act in the best interests of humans. The benefits of robotics should be available to all and be affordable.

The first principle is respect for fundamental human rights. A hospital service robot must not injure a patient due to poor maintenance or incorrect settings. Equally, a patient must be able to refuse care from a robot.

The second principle is that of precaution. Robots must be safe for society and the environment. Humans must be made aware that they are interacting with a robot.

The third principle is inclusiveness. Information about a robot's decision making process must be accessible. In particular, roboticists should be able to examine that a robot does not become biased in its decision making.

The fourth principle is accountability. The robot developer and manufacturer are accountable for robotics's social, environmental, and human health impact.

The fifth principle is safety. Human safety, health, physical well being, and rights must be considered. Disclosing the risks of robotics is a must as a manufacturer. Robot designers must strive to prevent users from using a robot for illegal purposes and modifying or commanding it to harm.

The sixth principle is reversibility. Suppose a robot finds itself in an unsafe or undesirable situation. In that case, it must be able to undo its actions to get back to its desired and safe position.

The seventh principle is privacy, as humans have a right to private life. Robots must keep personal information secure and obtain consent for private data use.

The final principle is to maximise benefit and minimise harm. The risk of harm while interacting with a robot should be no greater than life before the introduction of robots.

In this video, I introduced you to the principles of roboethics. Roboethics is intended as a guiding ethical framework for the design and use of robots. It is a code of conduct for anyone working with robots. Some of these principles cover the current everyday uses of robots, and others are aimed at an imagined future of science fiction.

The upcoming lessons will allow you to use your newfound knowledge of robot ethics, starting with some moral dilemmas for you to judge. Animations of robots in work scenarios will follow this, where you will have to identify ethical and legal concerns. Use the laws and principles of robotics to guide you.
