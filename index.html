From principle to practice

Whose values are in the system?

AI ethics asks what should happen when a system affects people, and who answers for the choices built into it. A useful review starts with people and consequences, then checks the data, safeguards, oversight and the route to challenge a decision.

Cui bono? Cui damno?

Who benefits? Who bears the loss?

01

LMS section 1 · start with a decision someone will live with

Ethics asks what should happen, who is affected, and why.

Imagine an employer using software to rank job applications. It may save staff time; it also changes who gets an interview. Before asking whether the software is accurate, ask what “good hiring” means, whose interests count, which evidence is relevant, and who can correct a mistake.

Four words get used loosely in this topic. They answer different questions, and moving from left to right the answer becomes more formal and more enforceable.

Hiring example · invented

A lawful tool can still deserve an ethical challenge.

Suppose an application-ranking system meets the data-protection rules that apply. That alone does not show its criteria are job-relevant, that applicants have a fair chance, that it is accessible, or that its benefits outweigh its effects. Equally, a team may have a strong ethical reason to avoid a practice no law bans. “Legal”, “ethical” and “good policy” answer related but different questions.

Why the course starts with robots

Robots make the problem easy to picture: a machine senses something, acts, and changes a person's options. AI systems do the same through less visible actions: ranking a CV, flagging a claim, recommending a loan decision, choosing which case a worker sees first. The system never takes responsibility by itself. People and organisations choose its purpose, data, thresholds, setting and response to failure.

Asimov's laws: a useful story, not an engineering solution

Asimov's laws come from his robot stories. Each law gives way to the ones above it. He later added a Zeroth Law that sits on top of the other three.

Why they fail as engineering rules: “harm” is vague. Is losing privacy harm? What about a small risk to one person to prevent a larger risk to many? Rules conflict, and a machine may not know what a person intended. A floor-cleaning robot, a hiring model and a military system need very different controls. In a real project, name concrete hazards, limits, decision owners, behaviour under uncertainty, how a human intervenes, and the evidence required before use.

02

LMS section 2 · roboethics, plus the OECD's five principles

Use principles to find where a system could affect people.

The course groups roboethics into eight principles for people who design, make, buy, deploy and use robotic systems. Read each one as a lens for spotting a concern. The team then has to turn that concern into requirements, tests, named owners and a way for affected people to be heard.

Three principles that are easy to confuse

Safety, precaution and reversibility all sound like “be careful”. The difference is when they act relative to a harmful event.

Limit: the eight principles are the LMS's teaching framework. A principle helps a team ask a better question; evidence and the applicable rules decide what action is justified.

A second framework: the OECD's five AI principles

The OECD's principles are a separate, international recommendation for responsible AI, first adopted in 2019 and revised on 3 May 2024. Each heading names a value to protect; the questions show what evidence to look for in a real project. Neither framework replaces checking the law that applies to a particular use.

1

Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being

AI should benefit people and the planet, not only make a task faster or cheaper. The OECD highlights human capability and creativity, inclusion of underrepresented people, reduced inequality and protection of natural environments.

  • Who gains time, money, safety or access? Who loses income, control, privacy or opportunity?
  • Could people be excluded because they lack access, have different needs or are poorly represented in the data?
  • What energy, materials, waste or other environmental effects come with it?

Factory: a robot arm may cut lifting injuries and raise output. Check whether workers lose hours, face faster work or get paid training for new roles, and whether the gains reach them.

2

Rule of law, human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy

Respect dignity, freedom, autonomy, equality, non-discrimination, privacy, diversity, social justice and labour rights throughout the system's life. People need real human agency and oversight, including when a system is misused or used beyond its purpose. The 2024 revision also addresses AI-amplified misinformation while protecting freedom of expression.

  • Could it affect access to work, care, services, safety or a person's choices?
  • Are people treated differently or monitored without good reason? Can they object, correct data or reach someone with authority?
  • Have affected people had a real chance to raise concerns?

Hiring: check whether historic labels encode past exclusion, whether each data field is necessary, and whether applicants can correct a record or challenge a rejection.

3

Transparency and explainability

Tell people when AI is involved and what it can and can't do. Give information suited to the audience about inputs and the factors behind an output, where useful and feasible. People harmed by an output need enough information to challenge it.

  • Would a user or affected person know AI was used, and what role it played?
  • Can you explain the relevant inputs, limits and reasons in plain language?
  • Is there a usable route to question, correct or appeal?

Hiring: “the computer rejected you” explains nothing. Say which parts of the application were considered, what the tool can't assess, and how to request a review.

4

Robustness, security and safety

A system should keep working as intended despite failures, changed conditions or attack. Assess safety and security across design, testing, deployment and operation, and build a way to override, repair or safely decommission a system that causes undue harm.

  • What happens with poor inputs, unusual cases, outages, cyberattacks or changed conditions?
  • Did tests use realistic users and settings? Do monitoring and incident response continue after launch?
  • Can people switch to a safe fallback, correct an error or stop it?

Hospital robot: it needs a safe response to a blocked corridor, a wrong item, lost network or an emergency, not just a good demo on an empty route.

5

Accountability

AI actors take responsibility in proportion to their role, context and ability to act. Manage risk across the lifecycle, with developers, data suppliers, deployers and users cooperating. Risks named include bias, rights, safety, security, privacy, labour and intellectual property.

  • Who approved the use, checked the evidence, monitors outcomes and handles complaints?
  • Who can change, pause or stop it, and what records let them investigate an incident?
  • Are supplier responsibilities and downstream risks clear?

In practice: “the algorithm did it” doesn't identify who has to fix a harmful result.

Apply all five to one proposal

Review an AI hiring ranker before collecting any applicant data.

  1. BenefitWho gains, who bears the cost?
  2. RightsIs data use and treatment fair?
  3. ExplainCan applicants understand and challenge it?
  4. TestDoes it work safely across roles and groups?
  5. OwnWho monitors, responds, can stop it?

First define the job-related purpose and compare a non-AI process. Then map affected people, check data relevance and rights, test error patterns and security, explain the tool's role and limits, and name the people who can respond. If the team can't answer one of these, record the gap and close it before deployment. This sequence is a teaching aid; the OECD doesn't prescribe it.

Sources, after the explanation: the eight principles are the LMS's teaching framework. LMS section 3 · two bad outcomes don't make an easy answer

A dilemma is a clash between reasons we take seriously.

The LMS uses the familiar autonomous-car case: the brakes fail, and the car must either continue or swerve. One path may injure passengers; the other may injure pedestrians. The point isn't that the car should calculate whose life matters more. It's to expose what people value when every option on offer is bad.

Two ways of reasoning about the fork

Reason A · consequences

Reduce the amount of harm

A utilitarian-style argument asks which action is likely to cause fewer deaths or injuries overall. It makes consequences visible, but counts and probabilities may be uncertain, and a smaller total can still put an unfair burden on one person or group.

Reason B · rights and duties

Respect rights and duties

A rights- or duty-based argument asks whether the system may deliberately expose someone to danger, whether people are treated equally, and whether the choice respects their rights. It resists sacrificing one person for a better total, but may not say what to do when duties conflict.

What Moral Machine can and can't tell us

The linked Moral Machine lets visitors judge stylised road dilemmas and compare their choices with everyone else's. That shows patterns in what participants chose. It can't establish one correct rule, represent everyone equally, or decide public policy: participants self-select, a small set of scenarios limits what can be chosen, and choices in a game aren't behaviour under real conditions.

When looking at a result, ask who was invited, who took part, how the choices were worded, which options were left out, and how disagreement is shown. A majority preference is never permission to violate rights or discriminate against a group.

04

LMS section 4 · the robot arm and the worker

Automation changes jobs, working conditions and who gets the gain.

The LMS animation shows a robotic arm moving cans from one conveyor belt into boxes. The arm can do a repetitive task consistently and keep people away from a hazardous line. The ethical issue is bigger than “robots take jobs”: the change affects safety, pay, control, skills, workload and who receives the productivity gain.

Replace, assist or redesign

These three words describe what happens to the task, not whether the outcome is good. None is automatically better; assess actual conditions before and after.

Illustrative scenario · not an LMS finding

A safer line can still be an unfair transition.

A factory installs a robot to lift heavy boxes. Injuries fall, but management also cuts a shift and brings in a dashboard that measures each worker's pauses. A review shouldn't stop at the injury figure. Ask who was consulted, whether jobs or hours changed, whether the monitoring is necessary and proportionate, how workers can contest inaccurate records, and whether the productivity gain funds retraining or safer roles.

Practical safeguards include consulting workers before buying, a job-impact assessment, paid training, redeployment options, safe staffing levels, clear limits on productivity monitoring, and a way to raise concerns without retaliation. If the robot reduces a hazard, record that benefit too. Be honest about both gains and losses.

Read after the explanation: the LMS automation activity asks you to consider company, worker and societal effects, the original work conditions, and how to reduce the disadvantages.
05

LMS section 5 · service, clinical support and human care

A useful robot can free staff time and still change how care feels.

The LMS lists hospital tasks such as delivering food and medication, taking temperatures, cleaning wards, answering common questions and carrying equipment. These aren't the same kind of system. A corridor delivery robot, a surgical assistant and software that suggests a diagnosis affect patients differently and need different controls.

Medication delivery is a workflow, not a journey

A robot carries labelled medicine from the pharmacy to a ward. A demo usually tests one step: getting down the corridor. The ethical review has to cover all five.

Dignity is practical

A patient may welcome a robot that brings supplies and dislike one that replaces conversation during a frightening stay. Needs differ by person, condition, language, disability and length of stay. Ask patients and carers rather than assuming. Keep a route to a person; make the robot's identity and limits clear; protect sensitive conversations; and don't make access to essential care depend on a difficult interface.

In clinical settings, reliability, maintenance, infection control, cybersecurity and training are safety issues. Privacy, consent, accessibility and human contact are ethical issues too. A system can do its narrow technical task correctly and still be unsuitable for the care process around it.

LMS loan scenario (section 8) · current UK law, explained before the links

Data protection covers information and its use. Automated-decision rules cover a narrower case.

What changes when an organisation uses personal information to make an important decision about someone without meaningful human involvement? Start with a situation. A lender receives a loan application; a computer checks the applicant's data, produces a low score and automatically refuses the loan. The applicant may not know which information mattered, whether it was wrong, or how to ask for reconsideration.

Don't confuse these four things

What data protection asks across the whole process

1Purpose

Why is each piece of data needed for this decision? Don't collect extra information just because it's available.

2Lawful basis

Which basis lets this organisation process the data here? A consent checkbox doesn't automatically make use lawful.

3Accuracy

Could the record be outdated, incomplete or linked to the wrong person? How is it corrected before harm follows?

4Transparency

Can the person understand what's used, why, and what the decision may mean for them?

5Security

Who can see or change the data? How are breaches, access and supplier handling controlled?

6Retention

How long is it kept, and why? Delete or anonymise it when no longer needed, subject to legal requirements.

7Rights and review

Can a person access relevant information, correct inaccuracies, object where rights allow, and challenge the outcome?

8Accountability

Can the organisation show what it decided about purpose, risk, suppliers, safeguards and ongoing checks?

Worked example · fictional loan decision

From automatic refusal to a reviewable process

Initial version: the system scores applications using repayment records, income and a postcode-derived feature. Below a threshold, the application is refused automatically. One problem could be an inaccurate record; another, a feature acting as an unfair proxy for disadvantage. The model's overall accuracy answers neither.

Run the four questions: personal data is processed. Credit access may have a significant effect, but the organisation must assess the law and facts rather than assume every score crosses the threshold. If no person meaningfully takes part before the refusal, it may be solely automated. These are separate checks.

Improved process: test whether each input is relevant and necessary; check source accuracy and group-level outcomes; give intelligible information; let the person correct information and explain circumstances; make a trained reviewer available with access to the case, time and authority to change the result. Record reviews and monitor outcomes. This makes the process more responsible; it doesn't by itself prove the lending criteria are lawful or fair.

“A human can override it” doesn't settle the question

A reviewer who sees only a red “decline” label, handles hundreds of cases an hour, has no access to the underlying information and is marked down for changing recommendations will probably rubber-stamp the system. People tend to defer to a system that looks objective; this is called automation bias, and training alone won't fix a workflow that rewards quick approval.

What the UK safeguards mean in plain language

For a significant decision based solely on automated processing, the amended UK GDPR framework requires safeguards including: information about the decision, a way to make representations, a way to obtain human intervention, and a way to contest it. The route has to be usable: the person needs to know where to go, how to explain an error or circumstance, and how a competent reviewer will reconsider. Keep a record of challenges and outcomes. If reviewers keep changing decisions for the same reason, fix the underlying data or rule rather than treating each correction as a one-off.

Human review has its own limits and costs: it exposes personal information to more people, and reviewers bring their own biases. Give reviewers only what they need, protect access, test the review process, and check that similar cases get similar outcomes. A safeguard needs monitoring too.

The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 changed this area of UK law. The relevant amendments took effect on 5 February 2026. For significant decisions using ordinary personal data, the framework is more permissive than the old rule in some circumstances, but the safeguards remain. Significant decisions using special-category data (such as health, racial or biometric data) face extra restrictions. Processing still needs a valid lawful basis and must meet the other data-protection duties. The details depend on the controller, data, decision and statutory conditions.

Data protection isn't equality law, consumer law, employment law or safety law. A process can meet a data-protection requirement and still produce a discriminatory or harmful outcome that has to be assessed under other rules. And “we use a human” doesn't answer whether the criteria are suitable.

Jurisdiction boundary: this describes the UK position as checked on 14 October 2026. EU data protection law has its own rules, and the EU AI Act is a separate regime. Don't carry UK tests or dates across borders without checking.

Sources, after the explanation: DUAA 2025, section 80 sets out the UK GDPR amendments and safeguards; the GOV.UK DUAA summary explains the policy change; the ICO DUAA overview summarises the amended position. The ICO page on meaningful human review is still useful for review design but is marked under review following the Act. See also the ICO rights overview for individuals.

LMS section 6 · support, targeting and control

“A human is involved” is not enough information.

The LMS separates unarmed support robots from systems that can acquire or engage targets, then distinguishes a human who must confirm an action from a human supervisor who can intervene. The label matters less than the authority, information, time and ability people actually have when force may be used.

Questions to ask of each arrangement.
Course categoryEthical questions
Unarmed robotCan it expose soldiers or civilians to new danger? Is the surveillance proportionate? Who acts on its sensor report?
Human-in-the-loopDoes the operator see enough context? Is there time to assess identity and uncertainty, or does the interface push a quick confirmation?
Human-on/over-the-loopCan the operator spot an error in time? How many systems is one person watching? Is a stop technically possible, and does it work in the field?
Illustrative control test

A target-classification alert arrives with uncertain data.

In a human-in-the-loop arrangement, the operator needs enough context to accept, reject or ask for more information before firing. If the screen shows only a confidence number and a countdown, the approval is formally human while the real decision is made by the system and the time pressure. In an on-the-loop arrangement, the supervisor also needs reliable alerts, authority to stop, a manageable workload and a communications link that works. An override that can't be used in time isn't control.

Responsibility is spread across command, procurement, system design, training, maintenance and operation. Spread out doesn't mean gone. Teams should define who authorises deployment, who checks targeting rules, who logs decisions, who investigates an incident and who can suspend operation. Military law and international humanitarian law are specialist subjects; this guide doesn't assess the legality of any weapon or operation.

The Future of Life Institute pledge linked by the LMS is an advocacy pledge against lethal autonomous weapons. It states a moral position and asks signatories to commit; it isn't a treaty or law. Keep campaign, ethical argument and enforceable rule distinct.

LMS sections 7–9 · make responsibility operational

Principles say what matters. Governance makes people act on them.

A governance committee isn't useful just because it exists. Its job is to make sure someone with the right knowledge, authority and evidence makes decisions before deployment and throughout use. For the LMS hiring system, that means examining the tool's role, who's affected, what data and criteria matter, which safeguards work, and how applicants can challenge an outcome.

Ethical question

What outcome can we justify?

Should the employer rank applicants this way? Is the data relevant to the job? Who may be excluded, and what benefit is worth the risk?

Governance question

Who does what, and when?

Who approves use, tests it, explains it to applicants, monitors complaints, fixes failures and can pause it? What evidence is needed at each stage?

A committee that can see the whole decision

Give the group access to evidence and a clear route to escalate or stop a risky deployment. Separate roles where practical: the person who benefits from deployment shouldn't be the only one deciding it's safe. Record disagreements and unresolved risks rather than smoothing them into a “green” status. Review the use case, not just the model card.

A repeatable risk review

Make one loan risk concrete

The LMS loan scenario asks how to avoid inadvertent discrimination and privacy violations. A risk record needs to be more specific than “bias risk: medium”. It should trace a route to harm and say what evidence would show the control works.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework

NIST's AI RMF 1.0 is voluntary guidance for organising how people find, test and respond to AI risks. It isn't a law, a safety certificate or an ethics score. Its core idea is that risk work runs across the system's whole life: before data is collected, while the model is built, during testing, in daily use, and when the system changes or is retired. It has four functions.

Frame the risk: what could happen, and to whom?

NIST treats risk as a combination of how likely an event is and how serious its consequences would be. A risk review isn't just multiplying two numbers. Name the event, the people or places affected, the route from system behaviour to harm, the evidence behind the estimate and the uncertainty that remains. Record benefits too, and ask who receives them.

Context sets the stakes

Same error, different consequence

A wrong product label is an inconvenience; a wrong medicine label can endanger a patient. An error rate alone can't tell you whether the risk is acceptable.

Metrics have limits

Missing evidence isn't proof of safety

Some harms are hard to count, affect a small group or only appear after deployment. Metrics can oversimplify, miss context or be gamed. Say what you couldn't measure.

Risk crosses suppliers

Check what others supplied

A vendor may test general performance while the organisation using it has a different population and purpose. Review third-party data, software, hardware, updates, and who owns each control.

The lab isn't the workplace

Recheck in real conditions

People, workload, lighting, devices and local habits change results. Monitor where the system is actually used, and listen to the people who live with its effects.

NIST also warns that people often assume AI works, and works well, in every setting, and treat it as more objective than a human. Risk tolerance isn't decided by the model. An organisation sets priorities, but must respect the law, rights, sector rules and affected people. Keep leftover risk visible after controls: “we added a safeguard” doesn't make it disappear. If serious harm can't be controlled, the answer may be not to build or deploy at all.

Who takes part, and when?

NIST, adapting an OECD framework, describes an AI system through four connected dimensions: the application context (where and why it's used), data and input (what it receives and where that came from), the AI model (how it processes information) and the task and output (what it recommends or does). Around them run the lifecycle stages. At the centre are People and Planet: human rights and the wellbeing of society and the environment.

NIST's Figure 3 spreads that wheel into columns and names who does what at each stage. TEVV, short for test, evaluation, verification and validation, runs through every column. The bottom row is not from NIST: it follows the hiring ranker through the same stages.

The people involved go well beyond whoever writes the code. NIST treats the People and Planet group (affected communities, advocates, researchers, standards bodies, environmental groups) as a separate audience that informs the builders: they supply context, point out actual impacts, help set boundaries on what the system may do, and push the trade-off discussion into the open.

For the hiring ranker, roles differ: an applicant can describe what a wrong ranking does to them; a recruiter can explain the workflow; a data scientist can test error patterns; a manager has to fund changes and own the go/no-go decision. Feedback matters only if it can change the design or the decision.

What does NIST mean by trustworthy AI?

NIST names seven characteristics to examine together. They're not boxes to tick once: a system can do well on one and fail badly on another. NIST says trustworthiness is only as strong as its weakest characteristic. Their layout carries meaning.

Valid and reliable

Does it do the intended job, with evidence from realistic conditions, over time? Accuracy should be reported with realistic test sets and broken down by data segment. A score from another population or setting may not transfer.

Safe

Could it endanger people, property or the environment? Risks of serious injury or death get the most urgent attention. Does it fail safely, and can people shut it down or intervene?

Secure and resilient

Can it resist attacks such as data poisoning or model theft, recover from disruption, and cope safely with unexpected change or misuse?

Accountable and transparent

Are roles, decisions, data origins, limits and incidents recorded and given to the people who need them? A transparent system isn't automatically accurate or fair, but an opaque one is hard to check.

Explainable and interpretable

Can operators understand how it works, and can a person make sense of what a particular output means for them? Related but different questions; see below.

Privacy-enhanced

Does the design limit intrusion and unnecessary data use, protect confidentiality, and reduce the risk of identifying people from combined or inferred data?

Fair, with harmful bias managed

Who is more likely to be missed, wrongly flagged or excluded? NIST names three kinds of bias: systemic, computational and statistical, and human-cognitive. Balanced predictions across groups can still leave the system inaccessible to disabled people.

NIST separates three ideas that people often blur together:

What?Transparency

What happened in the system, and what information about it is available.

How?Explainability

How the decision was produced: the mechanism.

Why?Interpretability

Why this output, and what it means in context for the person using it.

Trade-offs need reasons: stronger privacy measures can reduce the data available and cost accuracy; a more accurate model may be harder to interpret; security can make a system less convenient. Measurement can show that a trade-off exists, but not which value should win. The people responsible have to make that call openly and justify it.

The AI RMF Core: what the four functions ask teams to do

NIST's Core tables list detailed categories and outcomes for each function. The summary below turns the main work into questions a student can use. NIST says these actions aren't a mandatory checklist and needn't follow a fixed order; the process repeats as the system and its setting change.

GovernRules, roles and culture

  • Know and record the legal and organisational requirements.
  • Set policies, approval routes, review dates, a system inventory and clear owners.
  • Give staff the training, time, independence and authority to do the work.
  • Bring different disciplines and affected voices into decisions; record feedback and disagreement.
  • Manage suppliers and third-party data; plan how to update, switch off or retire a system safely.

  • State the purpose, users, setting, intended benefits, boundaries and prohibited uses.

  • Check the data suits the task and the target label measures what's claimed.
  • Map affected people and the likelihood and seriousness of benefits and harms, including misuse.
  • Include suppliers, laws, norms and non-AI alternatives; make an initial go/no-go call.

Hiring rankerDo past hiring records measure job ability, or repeat earlier managers' preferences?

  • Choose measures for important risks; say what can't yet be measured.

  • Check reliability, safety, security, privacy, fairness, explanations and human-AI interaction.
  • Where practical, use reviewers who didn't build it; consult domain experts and affected people.

  • Monitor suppliers and operation; provide ways to appeal, override and report problems.
  • Plan incident response, recovery, communication, changes, safe shutdown and retirement.

How would we know the framework is helping?

NIST doesn't claim that using the framework proves a system trustworthy. It asks organisations to check whether their risk work has improved. The benefits it expects:

  1. Clearer processes for governing, mapping, measuring and managing risk, with outcomes recorded.
  2. Better understanding of how trustworthiness characteristics relate and where they conflict.
  3. Explicit, documented decisions on whether to commission or deploy a system.
  4. Stronger policies and procedures that make people accountable for AI risks.
  5. An organisational culture that looks for risks and impacts on people and communities.
  6. Better sharing of risks, decisions, responsibilities, common mistakes, testing practice and lessons.
  7. More understanding of the setting and risks that may appear further downstream.
  8. Stronger involvement of people and groups who know the system or are affected by it.
  9. Better capacity to test, evaluate, verify and validate AI systems and their risks.

These are improvements in an organisation's process, not proof about a particular system. Check whether they actually happened and whether outcomes improved.

Use the questions on a new system.

A supermarket proposes a camera system that estimates which shoppers may need help at self-checkout. It might cut waiting and help staff respond sooner. It might also misread a customer, collect unnecessary images, affect groups differently or make shoppers feel watched. Work through the four prompts, then open the sample analysis.

  1. Name the decisionWhat does the system actually decide or recommend? What happens to a person because of it?
  2. Find people and missing viewpointsWho benefits, who is watched, who could be misread, who should test the process?
  3. Separate the issuesWhich concern is personal data, which is safety, which is unequal treatment, which is trust or dignity?
  4. Design a real safeguardWhat information, human authority, correction route, fallback and monitoring? What evidence would still make you reject it?
Open a sample analysis after your attempt

Purpose and decision: a discreet staff alert is different from a system that follows a shopper or labels them suspicious. The uses have different effects and data needs.

People and risks: shoppers, staff and the retailer are affected. Camera images may be personal data; differences in face, disability, clothing or movement could produce uneven errors. A false alert could embarrass someone or trigger an unjustified confrontation. The risk has to be tested, not assumed.

Controls: first ask whether a less intrusive approach meets the need. If cameras stay, define a narrow purpose, minimise and secure data, keep retention short, test with varied users, have staff treat an alert as a prompt rather than proof, give them a safe de-escalation procedure, and provide a way to raise or correct a concern. Monitor false alerts and complaints. Pause if it causes harm the team can't address.

Illustrative analysis, not a legal classification of a real product. The actual data, purpose, system and jurisdiction would need review.

Diagnose: “The model is 95% accurate, so the hiring process is fair.” What's missing?

One accuracy figure hides which errors occurred, for whom, and with what consequences. It says nothing about whether the target label is a good definition of job performance, whether the data is relevant, whether applicants can challenge errors, or whether a human meaningfully reviews the result. Ask for error types and impacts across relevant groups, the use context and the correction process.

Transfer: how does the same review change for a hospital delivery robot?

Same questions, different risks. Focus on collisions, blocked exits, wrong or late deliveries, infection control, privacy of labels and conversations, patient choice, accessibility, human contact and a tested manual fallback. The hiring system's fairness metrics don't answer these care questions.
