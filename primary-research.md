## Findings for a practical ethics overview

### 1. Ethics applies across the full lifecycle

UNESCO’s 2021 Recommendation treats ethical questions as arising throughout an AI system’s lifecycle, from research, design and development through deployment, use, monitoring, evaluation, and retirement. It grounds its approach in human dignity and rights, equality, diversity and inclusion, environmental and ecosystem protection, transparency, fairness, human oversight, and responsibility. UNESCO’s Recommendation is a **voluntary international standard**, not directly binding legislation. Its practical value is as a broad checklist for who may benefit or be harmed, including people not represented in the training data.

Source: [UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-ethics-artificial-intelligence) (adopted November 2021; official full text and scope).

### 2. Treat fairness as a question about both process and impact

The UK Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO) explains that data-protection fairness is not only disclosure or model parity: consider whether processing is reasonably expected, whether it causes unjustified adverse outcomes, and whether it is proportionate and justified in context. AI can reproduce or amplify bias, but statistical parity alone does not settle whether outcomes are fair. Check dataset relevance and representativeness, proxies and target choices, group-level and individual impacts, and whether people can obtain meaningful review or contest an outcome.

For personal data, the ICO says organisations need a lawful basis, should minimise data to what is needed, and must assess security, accuracy, transparency, individual rights, and accountability. Human review should be meaningful: a reviewer needs authority, information and competence to question an output rather than rubber-stamp it. The ICO’s AI and data-protection guidance is currently flagged as **under review following the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025**, so use it as useful regulator guidance but verify the latest chapter and law before relying on details.

Sources: [ICO AI and data protection guidance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/); [ICO fairness in AI](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/how-do-we-ensure-fairness-in-ai/); [ICO accountability and governance](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/what-are-the-accountability-and-governance-implications-of-ai/). The ICO overview states that its AI guidance is under review; chapter updates may lag behind legislative commencement.

### 3. Accountability means named owners, evidence, and recourse

A practical governance process should identify who owns the use case, who supplied or built the system, who approves deployment, who monitors performance, who investigates incidents, and who can stop or roll back use. Keep an auditable record of purpose, data sources, assumptions, tests, limitations, human-oversight arrangements, decisions, complaints, incidents, and changes. Provide an accessible route for people to ask questions, challenge consequential outcomes, and obtain human review where required. Accountability cannot be transferred to “the algorithm” or the vendor.

This operational framing is supported by UNESCO’s lifecycle responsibility principle, ICO accountability guidance, and the OECD AI Principles. It is an **implementation synthesis**, not a single legal checklist applying identically to every use case.

### 4. Risk management should be proportionate and iterative

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0) organises voluntary risk work around **Govern, Map, Measure, Manage**. Use it to make risk ownership continuous: define context and affected groups; identify foreseeable harms and misuse; assess likelihood and severity; test for safety, security, reliability, privacy and bias; decide mitigations and residual-risk acceptance; monitor in operation; and revise or retire the system when assumptions fail. NIST’s Generative AI Profile adds risks specific to generative systems, including confabulation, privacy, information integrity, harmful bias, intellectual property, security, and human-AI configuration.

NIST’s official page says AI RMF 1.0 is being revised; treat the framework as a voluntary tool and check for a newer version before a final guide is issued.

Sources: [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework) (framework page; revision noted); [NIST Generative AI Profile, NIST AI 600-1](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf) (published 26 July 2024).



### 9. NIST AI RMF 1.0: learner-facing extraction

The NIST AI RMF is voluntary guidance to help organisations and people manage risks through AI design, development, deployment, use and evaluation. NIST's current AI RMF pages say version 1.0 is being revised; check the live sources before later publication or a legal-status claim. The Playbook gives suggested actions, not a mandatory sequence.

**Risk framing and limitations.** NIST frames risk around the likelihood of an event and the magnitude of its consequences. AI can create positive opportunities as well as negative impacts. Impacts may fall on individuals, groups, communities, organisations, society and the environment. Context, uncertainty, risk tolerance and prioritisation matter; a single score cannot settle which harms matter or which trade-offs are acceptable. NIST highlights weak or unavailable metrics, third-party components and data, emergent and operational risks, lab-to-field differences, opaque systems, group differences, human baselines and residual risk. The absence of a reliable measure does not imply low risk. Serious unacceptable risk may require stopping the system until controls are adequate.

**Audience.** The Framework concerns actors across the application context, data/input, AI model and task/output dimensions and across plan/design, development, evaluation, deployment and use. Relevant actors include planners, designers, data suppliers, developers, evaluators, buyers, deployers, operators and users. People outside a project team—end users, impacted individuals and communities, advocates, researchers, standards bodies and environmental organisations—can explain context, identify effects and help define boundaries.

**Trustworthiness characteristics.** NIST describes valid and reliable; safe; secure and resilient; accountable and transparent; explainable and interpretable; privacy-enhanced; and fair with harmful bias managed. These characteristics are socio-technical, context-dependent and interrelated. One success does not establish overall trustworthiness. Trade-offs may include accuracy versus interpretability, or privacy-enhancing techniques versus accuracy under data sparsity. Human judgment and transparent justification are needed to resolve value choices.

**Effectiveness.** NIST calls on Framework users to review whether the RMF improves risk processes and outcomes. Expected benefits include clearer documentation, understanding of trade-offs, explicit go/no-go decisions, stronger accountability and risk culture, better information sharing, contextual awareness of downstream risks, stronger engagement with relevant actors and improved testing/evaluation capacity. These are goals to assess, not a certification result.

**Core functions.** Govern establishes policy, legal awareness, responsibilities, training, diverse decision-making, feedback routes, third-party controls and safe decommissioning. Map documents purpose, context, users, constraints, system task/method, data suitability, capabilities, limits, benefits, costs, affected people, impact likelihood/magnitude and dependencies; it supports an initial go/no-go. Measure chooses and documents quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods; tests realistic deployment conditions; assesses trustworthiness characteristics, uncertainty, group effects and control efficacy; uses independent review where possible; and tracks feedback and emergent risk. Manage prioritises and treats risk, records residual risk, considers non-AI alternatives, monitors operation and suppliers, and provides appeal/override, incident response, recovery, change control, deactivation and retirement. NIST says Core actions are not a checklist or necessarily ordered; governance cuts across the other functions and work iterates across the lifecycle.

**Graphic attribution.** The learner guide contains an accessible HTML/CSS redraw adapted from NIST AI RMF 1.0 Figure 5, “Functions organize AI risk management activities.” It preserves the key relationship: Govern is cross-cutting; Map, Measure and Manage connect and repeat. It is labelled as an adaptation, not presented as an official NIST image.

Sources: [NIST AI RMF 1.0 (NIST AI 100-1)](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/nist.ai.100-1.pdf); [Framing Risk](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/1-sec-risk/); [Audience](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/2-sec-audience/); [AI Risks and Trustworthiness](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/3-sec-characteristics/); [Effectiveness of the AI RMF](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/4-effectiveness/); [AI RMF Core](https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/5-sec-core/); [NIST AI RMF Playbook](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/nist-ai-rmf-playbook); [NIST AI RMF FAQ](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework/ai-risk-management-framework-faqs). ### 5. Be precise about the UK position

The 2023 UK AI Regulation White Paper proposed a cross-sector, regulator-led approach centred on five principles: safety, security and robustness; appropriate transparency and explainability; fairness; accountability and governance; and contestability and redress. The 2024 regulator guidance describes these principles as **non-statutory/voluntary guidance for regulators**. Do not present that 2023/24 framework by itself as a comprehensive AI Act or as a substitute for existing laws applying to particular sectors and uses.

A major later change is the **Data (Use and Access) Act 2025**. Its amendments to UK data-protection rules on significant decisions based solely on automated processing took effect in stages, with the key UK GDPR automated decision-making changes commenced on **5 February 2026**. The amended regime is more permissive for certain solely automated significant decisions, but requires safeguards, including information, a way to make representations and contest the decision, and human intervention. Special-category data remains subject to additional restrictions. The precise legal position depends on the type of processing and the operative provisions; avoid teaching the former blanket summary of UK GDPR Article 22 as current without this update.

Sources: [UK 2023 AI Regulation White Paper](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-regulation-a-pro-innovation-approach/white-paper); [2024 initial guidance for regulators](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/implementing-the-uks-ai-regulatory-principles-initial-guidance-for-regulators/implementing-the-uks-ai-regulatory-principles-initial-guidance-for-regulators); [Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, section 80 and commencement text](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/contents); [2026 Commencement No. 6 Regulations](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/82/contents/made); [government commencement tracker, updated 12 October 2026](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/data-use-and-access-act-2025-plans-for-commencement).

### 6. Be precise about the EU position

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (EU AI Act) is binding EU legislation with a risk-based structure, prohibitions, obligations for high-risk systems, transparency duties, and governance/enforcement arrangements. Its phased application is not the same as its entry into force. The Commission’s live status page, checked 14 October 2026, says it entered into force on **1 August 2024**; prohibited-practice and AI-literacy provisions applied from **2 February 2025**; governance and GPAI obligations from **2 August 2025**; and the general application date is **2 August 2026**, subject to exceptions. The 2026 AI Omnibus has changed high-risk transition dates: Annex III high-risk use cases are now scheduled for **2 December 2027**, and AI embedded in regulated products for **2 August 2028**. Confirm against the consolidated legislation and latest Commission page before publication; these dates have changed since course materials were likely recorded.

Sources: [EU AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng); [European Commission AI Act overview and application timeline](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai) (live page checked 14 October 2026); [Commission enforcement timeline](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/enforcement-ai-act) (last updated 6 October 2026).

### 7. International principles offer a useful concise framework

The OECD AI Principles were amended in **May 2024** to reflect generative and general-purpose AI. They group trustworthy AI around inclusive growth and wellbeing; human rights, democratic values, fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; and accountability. The revised material explicitly addresses information integrity, responsible business conduct across the lifecycle, environmental sustainability, and the ability to override, repair or safely decommission a system that causes undue harm. These Principles are an **intergovernmental recommendation**, not a directly enforceable law for an individual developer.

Sources: [OECD AI Principles](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/ai-principles.html); [OECD Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence, OECD/LEGAL/0449](https://legalinstruments.oecd.org/en/instruments/OECD-LEGAL-0449) (amended 3 May 2024).

## Suggested learner-facing synthesis (enrichment, not LMS quotation)

Before using an AI system, ask: **What is its purpose? Who could be affected? What data and assumptions shape its output? What could go wrong, including for groups underrepresented in the data? How will we test and monitor it? Who is responsible for the decision? Can a person challenge it and reach a competent human? What evidence would make us pause, fix, or stop it?**

Then repeat those questions over the system’s life: design, procurement, testing, deployment, monitoring, incident response, and retirement. The ethical choice may be not to deploy, or to narrow the use, when risks cannot be reduced or justified.

### 8. Explain UK automated decisions as a narrower data-protection case

The **Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, section 80** replaced the UK GDPR's prior Article 22 structure with new Articles 22A–22D. For this teaching explanation, keep four checks separate: (1) is personal data being processed? (2) is there meaningful human involvement in taking the decision? (3) does the decision have a legal or similarly significant effect? and (4) which UK GDPR conditions and safeguards apply to the data and use? The legal test concerns the decision process and its effects, not whether the software is marketed as AI. A simple automated rule can matter; an AI tool may only advise a person.

For significant decisions based solely on automated processing, the revised UK GDPR requires safeguards including information about the decision, a way for the person to make representations, obtain human intervention and contest the decision. Significant decisions relying on special-category personal data have additional restrictions. These points do not remove the general data-protection duties that can apply when a person is involved: purpose, lawful basis, fairness, transparency, data minimisation, accuracy, security, retention and rights remain relevant. Other legal regimes, such as equality or consumer law, are separate questions.

Meaningful review should be taught as operational capacity, not a button label. ICO guidance says reviewers should actively weigh and interpret the recommendation, consider the available input and additional relevant factors, and have authority and competence to disagree. The design should give them enough information and time. A reviewer who routinely approves a score without examining the case may not meaningfully influence the decision. The ICO's existing AI guidance is marked under review following DUAA; a March 2026 draft impact-assessment page documents work on updated ADM guidance, so do not present pre-DUAA operational guidance as a final statement of the amended legal rule.

Sources: [DUAA 2025, section 80](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2025/18/section/80); [GOV.UK summary of DUAA data-protection and privacy changes](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/data-use-and-access-act-2025-data-protection-and-privacy-changes); [ICO summary of DUAA data-protection changes](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/what-we-do/legislation-we-cover/data-use-and-access-act-2025/the-data-use-and-access-act-2025-duaa-summary-of-the-changes/data-protection/); [ICO practical guidance on meaningful human review](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/how-do-we-ensure-individual-rights-in-our-ai-systems/); [ICO ADM guidance update impact assessment, March 2026](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/research-reports-impact-and-evaluation/impact-and-evaluation/impact-assessment/update-to-automated-decision-making-guidance-draft-impact-assessment/).

## Updated current-law check, 14 October 2026

- **UK commencement:** legislation.gov.uk records section 80 automated-decision changes in force from 5 February 2026. New Articles 22C and 22D set safeguards and further regulatory powers. Check the operative text and case-specific scope rather than relying on the old Article 22 summary.
- **UK official summaries:** GOV.UK describes the change as a more permissive framework for significant solely automated decisions, with safeguards including information, representations, challenge and human intervention. The ICO's DUAA summary describes the special-category restriction and notes that other lawful bases may apply under the amended regime. Do not reduce this to “automated decisions are now unrestricted.”
- **ICO practical guidance:** the public rights explainer and AI guidance are explicitly marked under review following DUAA. Use them to teach review design only with a clear date/status caveat; the statutory text is the primary legal source.
- **EU comparison:** the European Commission's AI Act overview confirms, as checked 14 October 2026, that the Act's general application began 2 August 2026 with exceptions; certain Annex III high-risk requirements are scheduled for 2 December 2027 and product-embedded requirements for 2 August 2028 after the AI Omnibus. These EU AI Act categories are not the UK GDPR test for a solely automated significant decision.

### 10. OECD AI Principles and policy recommendations: expanded extraction

The OECD Recommendation of the Council on Artificial Intelligence (OECD/LEGAL/0449) was adopted in 2019 and amended on 3 May 2024. The OECD describes it as a values-based intergovernmental standard with five principles for responsible stewardship of trustworthy AI and five recommendations directed to policymakers. It is not, by itself, directly enforceable law for an individual student or developer. Applicable domestic law remains a separate check.

**Five principles for responsible AI.**

1. **Inclusive growth, sustainable development and well-being.** Seek benefits for people and the planet, including stronger human capability and creativity, inclusion of underrepresented groups, reduced inequality and protection of natural environments. In a project review, ask who gains and who bears costs, whose access or representation is missing, and what resource or environmental effects accompany the system.
2. **Human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy.** Respect the rule of law; dignity, autonomy, freedom, equality and non-discrimination; privacy and data protection; diversity, social justice and labour rights. Preserve human agency and oversight, including for misuse or use outside the intended purpose. The revised text also addresses AI-amplified misinformation and disinformation while recognising freedom of expression. For students, connect the principle to data necessity, affected groups, meaningful choice, oversight, worker effects and routes to raise concerns.
3. **Transparency and explainability.** Give information appropriate to the context about AI capabilities and limits, make AI interactions known (including at work), explain relevant data sources and factors behind outputs where useful and feasible, and provide information that enables people adversely affected to challenge outcomes. An explanation should be useful to the audience: disclosure that “AI was used” alone does not explain the relevant factors or offer a challenge route.
4. **Robustness, security and safety.** Make systems work reliably for their intended use, assess and manage safety and security across the lifecycle, and prepare to override, repair or safely decommission systems that cause undue harm. Ask how the system behaves with failures, attacks, unusual inputs or changed conditions, and what tested fallback and incident response exist.
5. **Accountability.** AI actors take responsibility according to their role, context and ability to act. Apply ongoing lifecycle risk management and responsible business conduct, including cooperation across developers, suppliers, deployers and users. Risks include harmful bias; human rights, safety, security and privacy; labour; and intellectual property. Name owners, preserve records, monitor effects and give someone authority to respond; do not attribute a harmful result to “the algorithm.”

**Five recommendations for policymakers.** These recommendations establish wider conditions around individual projects; they are not a student checklist of legal obligations.

1. **Invest in AI research and development.** Support public and private research, including interdisciplinary investigation of technical issues and social, legal and ethical implications. Support open datasets that are representative and respect privacy and data protection. Project translation: document evidence, data provenance, coverage, permissions and limitations; openness alone does not establish suitability.
2. **Foster an inclusive AI-enabling ecosystem.** Develop access to data, AI technology, computing and connectivity, and mechanisms for sharing knowledge; consider data trusts for safe, fair, legal and ethical data sharing. Project translation: record who can access infrastructure, who is missing from data, supplier dependencies and conditions for safe sharing.
3. **Shape an enabling, interoperable governance and policy environment.** Support transition from research to deployment through agile policy and controlled experimentation; use outcome-based approaches, cooperate across jurisdictions, and review policy and assessment frameworks as AI changes. Project translation: identify relevant rules and setting, bound any pilot, specify evidence and a go/no-go decision, and avoid assuming one jurisdiction's answer applies everywhere.
4. **Build human capacity and prepare for labour-market transformation.** Work with stakeholders to build people's ability to use AI; support skills, social dialogue, fair worker transitions, retraining, affected workers and job opportunities; promote safer work and good job quality and share benefits broadly. Project translation: include worker and user perspectives, assess job and workload changes, and identify training or support needs.
5. **Cooperate internationally for trustworthy AI.** Share knowledge and expertise through international, cross-sector and multi-stakeholder work; develop consensus-driven global technical standards and comparable indicators to understand progress. Project translation: test assumptions when a dataset or result moves across languages, populations and jurisdictions, and document what does not transfer.

The learner guide uses the principles as evaluative lenses and the recommendations as context for what helps projects succeed. Its applied sequence—purpose and alternatives; people and distribution of benefits/burdens; rights and data; explanation and challenge; testing and controls; named owners and stop conditions—is an instructional synthesis, not a sequence prescribed by the OECD. The hiring example and checklist are illustrative teaching adaptations.
