## 2. Roboethics

The LMS summary card describes roboethics as a general ethical framework for robotics, establishing principles for protecting humanity from robots and applying when robots are designed, produced and used. The video transcript is preserved separately in [`raw-transcript.md`](raw-transcript.md).

## 3. Moral dilemmas of AI

**Summary card:** A moral dilemma is a difficult choice where both courses of action may lead to harm. The LMS says these dilemmas matter where real-world problems require one of two actions and either may involve a moral failure.

**Visible lesson text:** The section introduces moral dilemmas of robotics and quotes engineer Karl-Josef Kuhn asking how researchers can equip a robot to react when choosing between two bad choices. It says ethics may not have one correct answer, and that some autonomous systems, such as cars, may face unexpected situations where they must minimise harm to passengers, other road users or bystanders.

**Challenge — Moral Machine:** The LMS introduces an external website created by university researchers to collect human perspectives on moral decisions. It says participation is optional and describes an opt-out from data collection. The visible instructions tell learners to start judging scenarios, open their descriptions, make choices, optionally withhold preferences, and compare their preferences with other participants. It says there are no more than twenty dilemmas and estimates about fifteen minutes each. The page also shows the headings Explanation, Extra Task, Solution and Review; their contents were closed and not captured.

## 4. Ethics of Automation

**Summary card:** Ethical dilemmas of replacing human labour; AI can work faster, more accurately and for longer than humans; the stated concern is the effect of mass unemployment on society.

**Visible lesson text:** The LMS moves from the autonomous-car example to the more everyday robotic manipulator, noting robotic arms are widely used across industries. An animation shows a manipulator transferring cans from one conveyor belt into boxes on another. The page points out that a person previously did that job.

**Challenge:** Learners are asked to consider whether replacing people with robots is ethical; whether the original work conditions were ethical; whether a tedious, repetitive job is worthwhile; and whether responsibility lies with the employer, the state or neither. The page then asks learners to consider pros and cons for the company, employee and society, and how to reduce identified disadvantages. It suggests about twenty minutes of writing and discussion. The headings Explanation and Review are shown, but their panels were closed.

## 5. Ethics of Healthcare Robotics

**Summary card:** Ethical dilemmas of replacing human labour in healthcare; robots may provide service and surgical assistance; human dignity is raised as a concern.

**Visible lesson text:** The section contrasts industrial automation with robots entering healthcare. An animation presents a hospital service robot. The page lists possible tasks: deliver food and medication, take temperatures, clean and disinfect wards, give updates and answer common questions, and carry medical equipment.

It says assigning some tasks to robots could allow nurses to focus on critical work, while also raising concerns: hospital stays can be traumatic, robot interaction could intensify that for some patients, and patients may value conversation with nursing staff. It says responses may differ between individuals.

**Challenge:** Learners are asked how they would feel receiving a mixture of robot and human service; whether robot service is acceptable for a short stay and for how long; whether visits from relatives change that view; and whether the patient’s condition should matter. It suggests about twenty minutes of writing and discussion. The headings Explanation and Review are shown, but their panels were closed.

## 6. Ethics of Military Robotics

**Summary card:** Ethical dilemmas of military robots; the card says they support soldiers and identifies reducing harm as a reason to consider the topic.

**Visible lesson text:** The LMS contrasts Asimov’s fictional laws with military applications and says the zeroth law can be read as allowing harm to an individual to protect humanity. It notes that the animation presents a possible future tracked military robot at a checkpoint, not a real robot.

The page groups systems into three categories:

1. Unarmed robots.
2. **Human-in-the-loop:** a robot may acquire targets, but a human must confirm before firing.
3. **Human-over/on-the-loop:** a robot may decide to fire, while a human could intervene and stop it.

**Challenge:** Learners are asked to consider advantages, ethical questions and drawbacks of these categories, including responsibility, accident risk and efficiency, then discuss their answers. The headings Explanation, Extra Task, Solution and Review are shown, but their panels were closed.

## 7. AI Ethics Governance

**Summary card:** AI ethics governance frameworks guide ethical AI implementation and are described as supporting compliance and ethical integrity.

**Challenge:** Imagine a company is about to deploy an AI system that could significantly affect hiring across departments. Learners are asked which ethical considerations should shape an oversight committee and how to align use with global ethical standards and local regulations.

The page shows the headings Explanation, Test your knowledge, Solution and Review, plus links under “Additional Perspectives From People We Like”: UNESCO’s Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence, the EU AI Act, and UK government material on AI ethics and safety. The closed-panel contents were not captured.
